Our Journey

From 1989 to today — building the future of sustainable transportation

1989

Project Founded

The Stanford Solar Car Project began in the winter of 1989, when a graduate student and a visiting professor believed Stanford students could take on the challenge of building a solar-powered race car. Inspired by General Motors' victory in Australia's World Solar Challenge, they aimed for Sunrayce '90 in the U.S. With little experience but plenty of determination, the students formed what would become one of the longest-running solar car teams in the world.

1990

SunSurfer

Sunrayce '90 7th of 32 teams; 2nd in performance-per-cost

Our very first car, SunSurfer, was built by an inexperienced but passionate group of undergraduates. Despite the late nights, epoxy fumes, and steep learning curve, the car successfully completed the race, marking Stanford's entry into the global solar racing community.

1993

Sunburner

Sunrayce '93 5th Place
World Solar Challenge '93 14th Overall, 2nd in 2-Person Class

A bold new design featuring a solar concentrator proved too ambitious, but the team adapted quickly, turning Sunburner into one of the most distinctive cars ever built. After a devastating fire nearly destroyed the vehicle, the team rebuilt it in just six weeks. Against the odds, Sunburner achieved a remarkable 5th place in Sunrayce '93 and went on to a strong showing in Australia.

1995-1997

Afterburner

Sunrayce '95 5th Place
Suzuka '95 5th Place
Suzuka '96 7th Place
World Solar Challenge '96 25th Place (as "Team California")
Sunrayce '97 3rd Place Overall
Suzuka '97 5th Place

With Afterburner, Stanford shifted focus to aerodynamics, cutting drag in half compared to its predecessor. The car matured over three years of racing, overcoming tough weather and fierce competition to earn Stanford's first podium finish — 3rd place at Sunrayce '97.

2001

Third Degree Burner

American Solar Challenge 2001 2nd in Stock Class, 15th Overall

Third Degree Burner marked a new era as Stanford once again raced independently. Despite limited resources, the team's careful preparation paid off with a strong 2nd place in class along historic Route 66.

2003

Back2Back Burner

American Solar Challenge 2003 2nd in 2-Person Class, 15th Overall

Pushing innovation further, Stanford built its first two-seat solar car. Back2Back Burner featured advanced aerodynamics and experimental lithium-polymer batteries. Though reliability issues plagued the race, the team persevered to finish and gained valuable lessons that would shape future designs.

2005

Solstice

North American Solar Challenge 2005 1st in Stock Class

Solstice delivered one of the team's proudest moments, winning the Stock Class in a 2,500-mile race from Texas to Canada. This victory showcased not only engineering excellence but also the resilience and spirit of the team.

2007

Equinox

World Solar Challenge 2007 Classified DNF

Stanford's Equinox was a leap forward in design, but tragedy struck when a slow tire leak caused the car to flip during the race. The team rallied, repaired the car in the Australian outback, and drove it to the finish line. Although classified as "Did Not Finish," the effort demonstrated the grit and perseverance that defines SSCP.

2009

Apogee

World Solar Challenge 2009 10th in Class

Built from carbon fiber with a sleek monocoque body, Apogee carried the team back to Australia. With a 6 m² solar array and lightweight lithium-ion batteries, it was one of Stanford's most reliable cars to date, cruising steadily across the continent to a top-ten class finish.

2011

Xenith

World Solar Challenge 2011 11th Place

Unveiled in August 2011, Xenith was Stanford's tenth car and a major leap forward in engineering. Weighing just 375 pounds, it featured a three-wheel steering system, a 4-inch-thin carbon composite chassis, and a custom 98% efficient motor. Xenith pioneered the use of flexible glass encapsulation for SunPower solar cells, with Corning providing prototype glass. In-house strategy software modeled sunlight and shadows in real time, giving the team a new competitive edge. Xenith could cruise at 55–60 mph under solar power alone.

2013

Luminos

World Solar Challenge 2013 4th Overall (Best U.S. Team)

Our eleventh car, Luminos, was unveiled in 2013 and went on to deliver the team's best result yet: 4th place in the Challenger Class and the top finish by an undergraduate team. Lightweight and incredibly reliable, Luminos logged over 10,000 safe miles, cementing its reputation as one of SSCP's finest designs.

2015

Arctan

World Solar Challenge 2015 6th Place

Arctan built on Luminos' success with a sleeker, more aerodynamically aggressive design. Extensive testing—more miles than any car before it—proved invaluable, helping the team close the gap to the leaders by 50% compared to 2013.

2017

Sundae

World Solar Challenge 2017 9th Place

When new WSC regulations reduced allowed solar cell area to 4 m², Stanford responded with Sundae, a compact and highly optimized design. Unveiled in 2017, Sundae was significantly smaller than its predecessors and marked a new era of rule-driven innovation. Despite fierce competition, it finished 9th in the Challenger Class.

2019

Black Mamba

World Solar Challenge 2019 DNF (Battery Fire)

Black Mamba was Stanford's fourteenth car and its first asymmetric "bullet" design. It represented a bold leap in aerodynamics and packaging. Unfortunately, during the 2019 race, a battery fire forced the team to withdraw. The driver escaped safely, and the incident provided valuable lessons that have informed every car since.

2021

COVID-19 Pandemic

The Bridgestone World Solar Challenge was canceled due to the COVID-19 pandemic, marking the first gap year in SSCP's racing history. Despite the challenges, the team continued development work and prepared for the future.

2024-2025

Azimuth

Formula Sun Grand Prix 2025 2nd Place (SOV Class)

After the pandemic, the team unveiled Azimuth, focusing on reliability and refinement. While it missed competitive running at the 2024 scrutineering, Azimuth came back strong in 2025, securing 2nd place in the Formula Sun Grand Prix with 212 laps (667.8 miles). Azimuth sets the foundation for Stanford's next push on the world stage.
